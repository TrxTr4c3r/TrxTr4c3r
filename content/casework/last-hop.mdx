## Published is not controlled

A site printing an address on its deposit screen proves one thing: that the site published it. It does not prove the operation holds the key, and it does not prove that money arriving there is victim money.

I tested that directly on a separate case. A fake trading platform handed over six deposit addresses across six chains, in a live chat with its own operator, which is about as good as provenance gets short of a confession. I traced all six. **Not one was operator-controlled.** Three had never been used at all. Three belonged to uninvolved third parties, including somebody's ordinary account at a regulated exchange, whose normal activity would read as collection volume to anyone who assumed the site owned the address.

So a fake platform publishing an address it does not hold is documented behaviour, not a theory. Every episode above now carries a control label, and so does every row of the [Scam Address Labels](/kb/casework/address-labels/) set:

| Label | What it means |
|---|---|
| **not established** | the site published the address, and nothing else about who holds it is known |
| **circumstantial** | on-chain behaviour points at the operation setting the address up. Still short of proof |
| **confirmed** | control demonstrated, for example a co-spend on a UTXO chain |

Nothing on-chain changes. Every amount, timestamp and hash in both episodes was read off a block explorer and stands. What changes is the sentence underneath them: an amount that arrived at a published address is a fact about that address, not proof of whose money it was. Episode 002 is the one this bites hardest, because both of its funded rails are exchange deposit addresses, and an exchange deposit address belongs to an account holder nobody has identified.

From episode 003 the control question gets answered before the trace is scored, by asking the operator for payment instructions directly rather than reading them off a deposit screen. One reply settles it.

**If you screen against this data**, treat a hit as a lead to check against your own account records. If a row matches one of your customers, the innocent explanation is live: a scam site can print a stranger's deposit address on its payment page, and that customer has done nothing.

## Why this is not casework

An episode is a fast public trace: entertainment, education, and a demonstration of method. There is no chain of custody, no exhibit hashing and no OSINT workstream, because nothing here is heading for a courtroom. If an episode turns out to matter, it stops being an episode and becomes a real case under the full [casework method](/kb/06-casework-method/).

Two things hold regardless. Every on-chain figure is explorer-verified before it is published, and no episode names or implies a person. Wallets and services get named. People do not.

## What a victim can actually do

Both episodes so far ended at an exchange, and that is the useful outcome, because a regulated venue holds account records. It is also where the honest answer gets uncomfortable: an exchange will not release those records to a member of the public, and once funds have been swept there is nothing left to freeze.

So the route is a report to your national police service, with the full addresses and the dates attached. The account record moves on a law enforcement request. That is what the write-ups are built to hand over.
