# Known Weaknesses to Disclose in a Forensic Report

<span style={{ fontFamily: 'var(--font-space-mono), monospace', fontSize: 13, opacity: 0.7 }}>`checklist`</span>

## Core idea
An analyst who cannot name the weaknesses in their own method is not an expert, they are an advocate. Six limitations sit under every crypto tracing analysis. Stating them plainly does not weaken the report. It is what makes the positive findings believable, and it removes the surprise from cross-examination.

## Components
1. **The pseudonymity gap: the methodology traces addresses, not people.** Every technique here operates on cryptographic addresses on a public ledger. Commingling analysis, clustering, change-address heuristics, chain-hopping, mixer tracing, DeFi forensics: all of them stop at the address. Connecting an address to a named person or legal entity requires evidence that exists off-chain. This is a structural feature of how public blockchains are designed, not a shortcoming of the analysis, but it has to be stated explicitly in every report.
2. **Heuristics are probabilistic, not deterministic.** Co-spend clustering, change-address identification, round-number analysis, fresh-address inference: each is right most of the time, under the conditions it was designed for, on the network architecture it suits. None is right every time. Courts increasingly expect the expert to quantify or at least explicitly characterise the reliability of each heuristic applied. A report that does not is vulnerable, not because the findings are wrong but because the basis for accepting them has not been disclosed.
3. **Data quality.** On-chain data is immutable and publicly verifiable, which is the forensic advantage. But an analysis rarely rests on chain data alone: attribution labels, exchange records, KYC data, IP information, and tool-generated clustering all enter at various points, and each carries its own quality risk. Data quality problems are among the commonest sources of error in reports produced under time pressure, and time pressure is a permanent feature of litigation work. Build verification in as a mandatory step, not a courtesy.
4. **The technology evolves faster than the method.** A methodology that was sound for Bitcoin in 2018 may not be sound for the same network today: CoinJoin implementations have matured, Lightning moves value into off-chain payment channels, and Taproot changes the on-chain footprint of certain transaction types. Currency of knowledge is itself a component of methodological rigour.
5. **Confirmation bias, or the brief becoming the methodology.** The structural risk of the engagement: you are instructed by one party, given that party's account of the facts, handed a starting point that reflects their interests, and asked to trace in a direction that, if their account is right, arrives at a predetermined conclusion. The discipline required is to test the hypothesis against the data rather than build a narrative around it. An analyst who follows only the path supporting the instructing party's case, without examining alternative paths the same data would support, has let the brief become the method. A competent opposing expert will ask why certain paths were not pursued.
6. **The interpretation gap: technical finding is not legal conclusion.** The analysis produces technical findings. Turning them into legal conclusions needs the expert's technical input *and* the court's legal determination. An expert who asserts rather than demonstrates, or characterises rather than describes, has stepped outside their function.

## When to use
As the limitations section of every report, filtered to the weaknesses that actually bear on this analysis, and as a self-audit before submission. It is also the reading list for the other side: see Reviewing an Opposing Forensic Report.

## Avoid when
Do not paste all six in as boilerplate. A generic limitations block reads as a disclaimer and carries no weight. Say which of these applies to this trace, at which step, and what it means for the confidence attached to the finding it affects.

## Example
"The clustering at step 4 rests on the common-input heuristic, which is highly reliable on Bitcoin but is defeated by CoinJoin. Two transactions in the set show a CoinJoin-consistent structure and have been excluded, and the cluster is therefore stated at moderate rather than high confidence." That is a limitation doing work. "Blockchain analysis is inherently probabilistic" is not.
