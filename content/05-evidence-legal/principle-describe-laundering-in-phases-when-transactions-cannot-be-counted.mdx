# Describe Laundering in Phases When the Transactions Cannot Be Counted

<span style={{ fontFamily: 'var(--font-space-mono), monospace', fontSize: 13, opacity: 0.7 }}>`principle`</span>

## Core idea
A trace across hundreds of thousands of transactions cannot be written transaction by transaction, and pretending otherwise produces a report nobody reads and nobody can check. The defensible alternative is to **describe the phases of the laundering and evidence each phase with a named sample, while stating plainly that it is a sample and why an exhaustive account was not possible.** Declaring the limit is what protects the analysis. A report that quietly presents a sample as if it were the whole is vulnerable in a way that an openly partial one is not.

## Components
- **Say what the volume is.** Give the real figure: number of transactions, number of addresses, value moved, and over what period. The scale is itself a finding, and it is the justification for everything that follows.
- **Name the phases.** Break the flow into stages the reader can hold: victim deposit, first-layer intermediaries, exchange accounts, second-layer intermediaries, consolidation, destination. Each phase gets its own section and its own evidence.
- **Give aggregate figures per phase.** How much entered each group of addresses and how much left it for the next. Aggregates are checkable even when individual transactions are not enumerated.
- **Evidence each phase with named examples.** One or two fully specified paths per phase, with real addresses, real amounts, and real dates. Label them as representative.
- **State the constraint honestly.** Say why full enumeration is not possible and what that costs the analysis. The 225M complaint does this explicitly, conceding that only *some* victim funds could be traced end to end, and listing the three reasons: transaction volume, consolidation addresses that redirect the tracing order, and intermediary addresses that both obstruct and redirect.
- **Keep the confirmed set separate from the inferred set.** Report interviewed-and-confirmed victims, back-traced-but-unconfirmed victims, and complaint-database matches as three distinct populations with three distinct counts. Never merge them into one number.

## When to use
Any case where the transaction count exceeds what you can list: high-volume exchange accounts, large mule networks, long-running scam infrastructure, or a scheme with more than a handful of victims. Also any case where the honest answer to "did you check every transaction?" is no.

## Avoid when
The case is small enough to enumerate. If there are forty transactions, list forty transactions. Sampling a tractable case looks like avoidance and invites the question of what the unlisted transactions would have shown.

## Example
The 225M complaint states its own limit in one sentence and then proceeds on that basis: "Due to the massive volume of transactions across a large network of addresses, this complaint describes the phases of laundering (including some specifics about relevant transactions) rather than detailing every single transaction."

It maintains the population separation throughout. Approximately 434 individuals were identified as suspected victims by back-tracing; approximately 60 of those were interviewed and all but one confirmed the scam; a further 42 victim reports were located in the IC3 and FTC complaint databases, with a footnote noting that some of those 42 overlap with the 60 interviewed. Three populations, three counts, and the overlap disclosed rather than hidden.
