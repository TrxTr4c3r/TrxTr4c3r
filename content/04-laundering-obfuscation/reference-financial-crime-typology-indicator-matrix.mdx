# Financial Crime Typology Indicator Matrix

<span style={{ fontFamily: 'var(--font-space-mono), monospace', fontSize: 13, opacity: 0.7 }}>`reference`</span>

## Core idea
Fifteen operational indicators crossed against eight typologies, showing which indicators are common, which are possible, and which are rarely seen. The value is not in the ticks, it is in the blanks: what is absent discriminates between typologies far better than what is present, because the common indicators are common everywhere.

**Reading it.** One isolated indicator means keep collecting evidence and avoid a premature conclusion. Multiple indicators inside a single typology means do more analysis and corroboration. Indicators spanning several typologies means carry multiple working hypotheses rather than picking one. Contradictory indicators mean reassess the assumptions and document the limitations.

**Five questions before naming a typology.** Which indicators are directly supported by documented evidence? Are multiple typologies reasonably consistent with what is observed? Which indicators are absent? Are there legitimate alternative explanations? Does the totality support the assessment?

## When to use
During the assessment phase, once the trace has produced enough observable behaviour to compare against. Also useful early, as a screening aid, to decide which lines of enquiry are worth opening.

## Avoid when
This is a decision aid, not a scoring system, and typologies are analytical frameworks rather than legal conclusions. The pitfalls: picking a typology off a single indicator, ignoring indicators that support a competing hypothesis, treating the matrix as arithmetic, assuming every observed indicator carries equal weight, and overlooking the limitations. Confidence tracks evidence quality, not the seriousness of the suspected conduct.

## Example
A case shows rapid movement, stablecoin conversion, exchange exposure, victim communications, withdrawal restrictions, and repeated investment solicitation. Those are common across investment fraud, pig butchering, and Ponzi schemes, so they do not discriminate. What discriminates is what is missing: no sudden liquidity removal rules out a rug pull, and no darknet interaction argues against a laundering-first framing. The question the matrix answers is which pattern the documented activity is most consistent with, not which crime you think happened.
