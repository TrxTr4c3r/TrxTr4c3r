# Illicit vs Licit Transaction Behaviour Profile

<span style={{ fontFamily: 'var(--font-space-mono), monospace', fontSize: 13, opacity: 0.7 }}>`tactic`</span>

## Core idea
Addresses used to move criminal proceeds behave differently in aggregate from addresses used to hold an investment, and the differences are measurable from the chain alone. This is a triage and prioritisation signal for deciding which of many candidate addresses to work first. It is not an attribution method and it does not survive contact with a single address considered on its own.

## Components
The tendencies the source reports, all population-level:
- **More transactions, smaller amounts.** Proceeds are moved; investments are held.
- **Repeat counterparties.** Illicit users transact with the same interlocutors more often, because the counterparties are suppliers, buyers, mules and services rather than a market.
- **Shorter holding periods.** The source reads this as consistent with an intention to avoid seizure. Value that sits still is value that can be taken.
- **A denser transaction web.** Illicit actors are more tightly interconnected through cross-transactions than licit users are, which is what makes community detection over the graph productive here and sterile elsewhere.
- **Obfuscation service usage raises the prior.** Mixer or CoinJoin activity, chain hopping, and atomic swaps all shift the probability, without in themselves establishing anything.

Four ways the source applies it:
- Track transactions out of the hot wallets of known illicit markets and mine the resulting customer address set.
- Segment addresses by these behavioural differences rather than working the whole set uniformly.
- Explore the network topology to identify communities of users from the transactions between them.
- Cross-reference against dark web forum material naming addresses, pseudonyms and nicknames.

## When to use
When you have more candidate addresses than you can work and need an order of attack. Also when scoping a cluster, where an address whose behaviour looks like custody rather than movement is more likely to be a service or an investor than an operator.

## Avoid when
Never as evidence about an individual. A high-frequency, low-value, repeat-counterparty pattern also describes a small merchant, a trading bot, a payroll wallet and a faucet. This is exactly the situation Single Indicator Is a Lead, Not Proof is written for, and the underlying study is a statistical estimate over a historical Bitcoin dataset, not a classifier you can point at an address today. It belongs in the analytical reasoning that directs work, not in a findings section.

## Example
A trace produces 60 downstream addresses. Sorting them by transaction count against average value and holding period puts a small group of high-frequency, low-value, repeat-counterparty addresses at the top. Those get worked first, and the long-dormant high-value ones are triaged as likely custody. The sort was a work-allocation decision, and nothing about it appears in the report as a finding.
